# Oscillator waveforms: the 8 shapes as data

Sylenth1's oscillators offer 8 waveforms: Saw, Pulse, HPulse, QPulse, Triangle, Sine, Noise, and TriSaw. This page explains how they are built on the band-limited minBLEP oscillator chosen in D6 ([band-limited-oscillators.md](band-limited-oscillators.md); read that first). Every periodic waveform is a short list of *corners*, so adding one is a row of data rather than new code. The page then lists each waveform's corners and reports how every waveform measures. The controls around the oscillator (tuning, volume, pan, and invert) are in [oscillator-controls.md](oscillator-controls.md).

## Theory

### Any piecewise-linear waveform is jumps and bends

Write one cycle of a waveform, phase t ∈ [0, 1), as straight segments. Where one segment ends and the next begins, the waveform can change in two ways:

- **Jump:** its value steps by J (the value after minus the value before).
- **Bend:** its slope steps by K (in units per cycle).

Between corners the waveform is a straight line, which has no high-frequency content. All the aliasing comes from the corners. A jump's harmonics fall as 1/k, and a bend's as 1/k². Band-limiting the waveform therefore means correcting each corner: a band-limited step residual scaled by J at each jump, and a band-limited ramp residual scaled by K·Δt (the slope change per sample) at each bend. The saw, the pulses, the triangle, and the TriSaw are all of this form.

Two consistency rules hold for any periodic waveform, and the tests check both:

- **Value:** the jumps undo the segments' net climb, so Σ J + Σ slope × length = 0.
- **Slope:** the bends cancel, so Σ K = 0.

### The waveform's Fourier series

The measurements compare each waveform's harmonics with its ideal Fourier series ([oscillator_support.hpp](../../tests/dsp/oscillators/oscillator_support.hpp)). The amplitude of harmonic k:

| Waveform | Harmonic k | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Saw, 2t − 1 | 2/(πk) | every harmonic |
| Pulse of width w, ±1 | (4/(πk))·\|sin(πkw)\| | zero wherever kw is a whole number: the square has no even harmonics; the 25% pulse has no 4th, 8th, …; the 12.5% pulse has no 8th, 16th, … |
| Triangle | 8/(π²k²) | odd harmonics only |
| Sine | 1 at k = 1 | |
| TriSaw, (triangle + saw)/2 | ½·√((2/(πk))² + (8/(π²k²))²) | The saw's series is all sines and the triangle's all cosines, so they add in quadrature. Falls as 1/k (the jump), with the odd harmonics boosted by the triangle. |

A pulse of width w has a mean (DC) of 2w − 1. That is −0.5 for the 25% pulse and −0.75 for the 12.5% pulse. The other waveforms have none.

## How it is applied here

### The waveforms

Names and shapes come from the feature inventory ([sylenth1-features.md §2](../reference/sylenth1-features.md#waveforms)). Three of them (HPulse's width, QPulse's width, and TriSaw's shape) are **working hypotheses**, to be measured against the demo in M1.13 (W2, W3). The narrow pulses are kept at ±1 rather than shifted to zero mean (W4, also open).

| Waveform | Segments (start: from → to) | Corners: jump J, bend K (per cycle) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saw | 0: −1 → +1 | t = 0: J = −2 | |
| Pulse | 0: +1; ½: −1 | t = 0: J = +2; t = ½: J = −2 | 50% |
| HPulse | 0: +1; ¼: −1 | t = 0: J = +2; t = ¼: J = −2 | width provisional |
| QPulse | 0: +1; ⅛: −1 | t = 0: J = +2; t = ⅛: J = −2 | width provisional |
| Triangle | 0: −1 → +1; ½: +1 → −1 | t = 0: K = +8; t = ½: K = −8 | |
| Sine | none; a sine of level 1 | none | |
| TriSaw | 0: −1 → ½; ½: ½ → 0 | t = 0: J = −1, K = +4; t = ½: K = −4 | shape provisional |
| Noise | not periodic | none | generated by `WhiteNoise` |

Phase 0 is where each waveform starts after a reset: the saw at −1 and rising, the pulses high, the triangle at −1, the sine at 0 and rising. This is the working hypothesis W6. Every waveform peaks at ±1 before band-limiting (W5, equal peak).

The TriSaw was chosen from three candidates (inventory W3) as the sum of a triangle and a saw, halved so that it peaks at 1. Of the three, it is the one that sounds most clearly unlike the triangle, since its jump gives it a saw's 1/k harmonics. It also exercises a jump and a bend at the same corner. Its mean is zero.

### Waveforms as data: `WaveShape`

[waveform.hpp](../../libs/dsp/include/anoesis/dsp/oscillators/waveform.hpp) describes each waveform as a `WaveShape`:

- **Corners.** Up to four `WaveCorner`s, each recording its phase (fixed point), the naive value and slope just after it, and its jump J and bend K. Two flags, `jumps` and `bends`, let the oscillator skip a residual that would be zero. (Comparing floats with zero would also trip `-Wfloat-equal`.)
- **A sine level.** A sine has no corners, so it is carried as a separate term.

Shapes are written as straight segments (`WaveSegment{start, from, to}`) and converted by `WaveShape::piecewiseLinear`, a `constexpr` builder. The builder works out the slopes, jumps, and bends in double precision at compile time, treating the segment before the first as the last one (the waveform is periodic). The 8 entries sit in one `constexpr` table, `waveShapes`, indexed by `Waveform`. An out-of-range index (a corrupt parameter value) gets the saw rather than reading past the table.

The oscillator's per-sample loop never names a waveform. A first version of `MinBlepOscillator::next()` ([min_blep.hpp](../../libs/dsp/include/anoesis/dsp/oscillators/min_blep.hpp)) did all of the following on every sample: it looped over the corners to test each for a crossing, searched for the current segment, and kept the sine's phase history. At ordinary pitches that made it about 60% slower than D6's hard-coded switch (4.7 against 2.9 ns per oscillator-sample for a bank of 32 saws), although almost no sample crosses a corner. So the hot path is now built around the common case:

1. **One compare per sample.** The oscillator keeps the distance, in phase units, from the previous sample's phase to the next corner. If the step just taken is shorter, nothing was crossed: subtract the step and carry on.
2. **The slow path, only when a corner was crossed** (or after a reset or a new shape). For each corner crossed, add J × the step residual and K·Δt × the ramp residual at the crossing's sub-sample position, using the same crossing test as before (`addCrossing`, which the band-limited phase jump also uses: [oscillator-phase.md](oscillator-phase.md)). Then cache the segment the phase is now in, and recompute the distance to the next corner. A corner exactly at the phase counts as just crossed, so its next crossing is a whole cycle (2³² units) away.
3. **The naive value and slope** come from the cached segment.
4. **Two versions of the loop body,** one with the sine and one without, chosen per sample by a single well-predicted branch. With the sine's history and `std::sin` in the body, even with the branch never taken, a saw sample cost 30% more on Apple clang. The extra code changed how the compiler allocated registers for the whole body.

So a new piecewise-linear waveform is a new table row. `setShape()` also accepts a shape built at run time, which leaves room for Part 2. The [PolyBLEP reference](../../tests/dsp/oscillators/reference/poly_blep.hpp) reads the same table, so the report compares like with like.

### The sine: delayed by its phase, not by its slope

minBLEP's delay compensation (subtract slope × τ, where τ is the kernel's group delay) is exact only for straight lines. Applied to a sine, it would give sin x − τω cos x: a sine with the wrong amplitude, √(1 + (τω)²). That is +0.1 dB at 440 Hz but ×2 at 5 kHz. So the sine part is evaluated at the phase τ samples ago instead. This keeps it aligned with the other waveforms: a sine and a saw at the same pitch start their cycles together.

That phase comes from a short history of the last 8 phases. Between two samples, the phase moves linearly by the increment that carried it, which is the same assumption the edge timing makes. So the phase at time n − τ is an exact interpolation between the two stored phases around it. This holds under per-sample pitch changes too, not just at a steady pitch. A test checks it under a fast, deep vibrato. Each entry also keeps the step that led to it, and, after a phase jump, where the old trajectory would have been, so the delayed sine switches to the new phase exactly when the jump comes out of the delay ([oscillator-phase.md](oscillator-phase.md#why-the-sine-needs-different-residuals)).

The **onset after a reset** remains band-limited for the straight segments, which start from silence with a step and a bend residual as before. For the sine, only the step is band-limited. The first sample removes the delayed sine's value with a step residual, so the output still starts at exactly 0, but the sine's slope at the onset is not corrected. The ramp residual cannot be used for this, because it carries the delay compensation that the sine doesn't use. Correcting the slope exactly would need the sine's higher derivatives too, since a sine is not a straight line near its onset. The cost is a small, one-off, non-band-limited bend at each reset, and an onset that overshoots to 1.96 for a sine at 19 kHz.

### Noise lives alongside the oscillator, not in it

`Waveform::noise` is in the enumeration, because the selector offers it, but `MinBlepOscillator` treats it as silence and `isPeriodic()` returns false for it. Noise comes from `WhiteNoise` ([white-noise.md](white-noise.md)). The reasons:

- **Nothing in the oscillator applies to it.** Noise has no phase, pitch, edges, delay, or onset.
- **It would cost every voice.** The random generator's state and a branch would sit in every unison voice of every oscillator, all to serve one waveform.
- **Unison handles it differently.** With noise selected, the oscillator slot runs one noise source instead of eight detuned copies, because detune and phase mean nothing to noise. It mixes two noise draws per sample to the power and the left/right correlation that N noise voices would have at the voices' gains, so Voices and Stereo still apply. The slot picks between noise and the periodic oscillators once per block, not once per sample ([oscillator-unison.md](oscillator-unison.md#noise-one-source-that-sounds-like-many)).

Whether Sylenth1's noise is white, uniform, and independent of the note is still open (inventory W7). `WhiteNoise` is white and uniform, with a peak of 1, which matches the equal-peak hypothesis W5.

### No pulse-width parameter

Sylenth1 has no pulse-width control. Its pulse widths are fixed per waveform, and PWM is done by summing a saw and an inverted saw (manual p. 15). So `setPulseWidth()` and `widthToPhase()` were removed. The two-saw trick is tested in [oscillator-controls.md](oscillator-controls.md#invert-and-pwm-from-two-saws). A variable-width pulse in Part 2 ("true PWM" is in its backlog) would be a shape built at run time and passed to `setShape()`.

### Beyond straight lines (Part 2)

Corners plus a sine cover every classic analog shape. Sampled single-cycle waves, such as the Korg DW8000's or the Ensoniq ESQ-1's (in the Part 2 backlog), are not piecewise linear, so they need a different source (a band-limited wavetable) next to this oscillator rather than more corners.

## Results

From the `[d6-report]` run ([antialiasing_report.cpp](../../tests/dsp/oscillators/antialiasing_report.cpp), 41 s in `relwithdebinfo`), with the method from [band-limited-oscillators.md](band-limited-oscillators.md#how-it-was-measured):

- **Renders:** every MIDI note 0–127, at a coherent frequency, with no oversampling.
- **Aliasing:** the worst alias below 20 kHz, relative to the fundamental.
- **Droop:** every harmonic up to 20 kHz compared with the waveform's Fourier series. Harmonics on a null of the series are skipped.

The saw, square, and triangle results are the same as D6's to the decimal: generalising the oscillator changed nothing.

- **The pulses peak highest,** because two jumps close together add their overshoots.

- **Stability:** every waveform stays finite and bounded from 0.01 Hz to Nyquist at both rates (in the tests, and in this table).

### Memory and CPU

- **Memory:** a minBLEP 64 oscillator is 392 bytes, up from 304 (264 at 32 taps, up from 176). The extra 88 bytes are the sine's phase history (32), the cached segment and the distance to the next corner (24), and the shape pointer, sine level, and flags. For 512 voices that is 196 KB. A PolyBLEP oscillator is 48 bytes, up from 36.
- **CPU** ([baselines](../performance/baselines.md); ns per sample, 64 taps, 48 kHz):

| | Saw | Pulse | HPulse | QPulse | Triangle | Sine | TriSaw |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 440 Hz | 3.2 | 3.7 | 3.6 | 3.7 | 3.9 | 7.7 | 3.9 |
| 12 kHz | 11.8 | 23.6 | 24.0 | 24.2 | 23.7 | 6.8 | 32.6 |

- **Cost follows the residual adds per cycle,** not the waveform. Each add is 64 multiply-adds. The saw has 1 per cycle; the pulses and the triangle have 2; the TriSaw has 3, since its corner at 0 both jumps and bends.
- **The TriSaw at 12 kHz is the new worst case.** 512 voices of it would take about 80% of a core.
- **The sine is the cheapest at 12 kHz and the dearest at 440 Hz:** it has no edges, but `std::sin` costs about 4 ns.
- **Against D6's hard-coded switch** (the parent commit's build, measured in the same session): the same within noise at ordinary pitches (32 saws: −4%), and 5–12% slower at 5–12 kHz and with the pitch set every sample.

## Trade-offs and alternatives

- **A table of data against a switch.** Reading the waveform from data rather than from constants in the code costs nothing on most samples, with the hot path above. It costs 5–12% where corners come often. The PolyBLEP reference, which tests every corner on every sample, pays more (25–110%), but it is a test-only reference and its quality numbers are unchanged.
- **The sine through `std::sin`.** It is exact (its harmonics are below −140 dB in float), and costs about 4 of the sine's 7.7 ns per sample. A polynomial sine would be faster, but at −100 dB it needs about degree 9. This is a Phase 3 question.
- **Combined residuals.** The TriSaw's corner at 0 adds two residuals (step and ramp) in two passes over the 64 taps. Doing both in one pass would save loads and stores (Phase 3).
- **Overshoot.** Narrow pulses at high notes peak higher than the saw (HPulse 2.06 at 10.8 kHz and 44.1 kHz). Most of this is the band-limited waveform itself, not minBLEP's overshoot: at 10.8 kHz only two harmonics remain, swinging around the HPulse's DC of −0.5. The filter's drive stages will see it either way.

## Tests

- [waveform_test.cpp](../../tests/dsp/oscillators/waveform_test.cpp), the table itself:
  - Every periodic waveform's shape matches its ideal form, which is written out independently in the test support.
  - Its corners sit where the ideal waveform jumps or bends, with the right values, slopes, jumps, bends, and flags.
  - Over one cycle, the jumps undo the segments' climb and the bends cancel.
  - The builder gets a skewed triangle's slopes and bends right, and `cornerAt` picks the right segment at, just before, and just after each corner.
  - Noise is silence in the table and not periodic.
  - An out-of-range waveform falls back to the saw.
- [band_limited_oscillator_test.cpp](../../tests/dsp/oscillators/band_limited_oscillator_test.cpp) runs every shared oscillator test on all 7 periodic waveforms, at minBLEP 32 and 64 taps and both PolyBLEP references:
  - The output follows the ideal waveform away from the corners.
  - It stays finite, bounded, and free of denormals from 0 Hz to fs.
  - Each waveform has its ideal DC.
  - Re-setting the same increment changes nothing.
  - `reset()` reproduces the output.
  - New tests:
    - The sine is a pure tone, with every other harmonic below −100 dB.
    - The minBLEP sine is the sine of the edges' phase trajectory delayed by `latencySamples()`, under a fast vibrato (to 10⁻⁴).
    - minBLEP starts every waveform at exactly 0 after a reset, at pitches from 50 Hz to 23 kHz.
- [antialiasing_report.cpp](../../tests/dsp/oscillators/antialiasing_report.cpp) (`[d6-report]`, hidden) prints the tables above.
- [oscillator_benchmark.cpp](../../tests/dsp/benchmarks/oscillator_benchmark.cpp) (`[oscillators]`, hidden) times every waveform at 440 Hz and 12 kHz.
